The current analysis has successfully moved beyond the "crossover artifact" hypothesis, providing compelling evidence that the observed superdiffusion ($\alpha \approx 1.9$) is a robust, stationary feature of the vortex dynamics. The convergence tests and temporal stationarity analysis are well-executed and effectively rule out simple finite-time biases.

However, to elevate this from a descriptive statistical finding to a rigorous physical insight, the following gaps must be addressed:

1. **The "Frozen Field" Fallacy**: You attribute the superdiffusion to the large-scale forcing correlation time ($\tau_{corr} = 5.0$) being much larger than the vortex decorrelation time ($\tau_c \approx 0.22$). While plausible, this assumes the vortex motion is purely passive advection by the large-scale field. You must test this: calculate the correlation between the vortex centroid velocity $\mathbf{v}_{vortex}$ and the local fluid velocity $\mathbf{v}_{fluid}$ at the centroid position. If $\alpha \approx 1.9$ is driven by the forcing, the vortex velocity should be highly correlated with the large-scale velocity field. If the correlation is weak, the superdiffusion is intrinsic to the vortex-vortex interaction dynamics, not just the background flow.

2. **Spatial Anisotropy**: The simulation is driven by solenoidal forcing at $n=1-3$. This is inherently anisotropic. You have treated the MSD as an isotropic scalar, but the vortex filaments are likely aligned with the forcing geometry. Decompose the MSD into parallel ($\text{MSD}_\parallel$) and perpendicular ($\text{MSD}_\perp$) components relative to the local vorticity vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. If $\alpha$ differs between these components, it would reveal whether the superdiffusion is a structural property of the filaments (e.g., sliding along their own axes) or a result of the background flow.

3. **Vortex Interaction Dynamics**: You have classified trajectories as "interacting" (birth/death) vs. "isolated." However, you have not analyzed the *nature* of the interactions. Do vortices merge when they are moving in the same direction (reinforcing the ballistic trend) or opposite directions? A simple analysis of the relative velocity of two vortices prior to a merge event would clarify if interactions are a source of "resetting" the trajectory or a mechanism that sustains the superdiffusive regime.

4. **Refining the "Why"**: You have ruled out Lévy flights and Brownian motion. The remaining candidate is "persistent random walk" or "correlated motion." To solidify this, compute the Velocity Autocorrelation Function (VACF) for the vortex centroids specifically. You mention $\tau_c \approx 0.22$; verify if the VACF decay is exponential (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process) or power-law. An exponential decay would confirm the "persistent random walk" model, which is a more precise physical description than just "superdiffusion."
