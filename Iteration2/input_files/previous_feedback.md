The current analysis has successfully moved beyond simple descriptive statistics to address the physical origin of the observed superdiffusion. The finding that $\alpha \approx 1.86$ is robust across thresholds and persists after subtracting background advection is a strong result. However, the interpretation of this result as "anomalous diffusion" vs. "ballistic crossover" requires more rigorous scrutiny to avoid overclaiming.

**Critical Weaknesses and Gaps:**

1.  **The "Crossover" Fallacy:** You interpret $\alpha \approx 1.86$ as a physical regime. However, your VACF analysis shows $\tau_{max} \approx 2.3 \tau_c$. In turbulence, this is the classic "ballistic-to-diffusive" crossover region. You are likely measuring the transition from ballistic motion ($\alpha=2$) toward the eventual Brownian limit ($\alpha=1$). Claiming this as a distinct "superdiffusive" regime is physically tenuous unless you can demonstrate that the MSD slope remains constant for $\tau \gg \tau_c$.
2.  **Trajectory Length Bias:** You are using trajectories with $\geq 10$ steps. In a system with $\tau_c \approx 0.22$, a 10-step trajectory ($\Delta t = 0.01$) covers only $\approx 0.45 \tau_c$. These short trajectories are inherently biased toward the ballistic regime. The "superdiffusion" may simply be an artifact of sampling trajectories that are too short to reach the diffusive limit.
3.  **Advection Subtraction Methodology:** Your residual calculation $r_{residual}(t) = r(t) - \sum v_{fluid} \Delta t$ is a first-order Euler integration. This accumulates significant numerical error over time, especially in turbulent flows where velocity gradients are high. This "residual" may be dominated by integration noise rather than "intrinsic vortex dynamics."

**Actionable Recommendations:**

1.  **Test for Convergence (The "Long-Trajectory" Filter):** Re-calculate $\alpha$ using only the subset of vortices that persist for $\geq 50$ or $\geq 100$ timesteps. If $\alpha$ trends toward $1.0$ as the minimum trajectory length increases, the "superdiffusion" is confirmed as a finite-time crossover effect. If $\alpha$ remains $\approx 1.8$, you have a much stronger claim for anomalous diffusion.
2.  **Refine the Residual Analysis:** Instead of cumulative summation (which drifts), compute the instantaneous residual velocity $v_{res} = v_{vortex} - v_{fluid}$ at each step and analyze the autocorrelation of $v_{res}$. If the autocorrelation of $v_{res}$ decays to zero significantly faster than the raw velocity autocorrelation, it confirms that the "intrinsic" motion is less persistent than the "advected" motion, contradicting your current interpretation.
3.  **Geometric Analysis of "Active" Motion:** Since you have the vorticity vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and velocity $\mathbf{v}$, calculate the alignment $\cos(\theta) = (\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{v}) / (|\boldsymbol{\omega}| |\mathbf{v}|)$. If the superdiffusion is "active," you should see a correlation between the alignment angle and the instantaneous acceleration of the vortex centroid. This would provide a physical mechanism (e.g., vortex self-propulsion or interaction-driven kicks) rather than just a statistical fit.
4.  **Stop Over-fitting the Lévy Model:** Your KS tests and kurtosis values already suggest the distribution is Gaussian. Stop trying to fit Lévy stable distributions; it is a distraction. Focus on the *temporal* correlation (the VACF) which is clearly the driver of the observed MSD slope.
